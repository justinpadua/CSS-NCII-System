[46] INTRODUCTION

[47] Computer Systems Servicing (CSS) is a technical field that involves installing, maintaining, repairing, configuring, and troubleshooting computer systems and networks. It is an important skill in today's technology-driven world because computers are used in education, business, communication, entertainment, and many other industries.

[48] This module introduces the fundamental knowledge and practical skills needed in computer servicing. Students will learn to identify computer components, understand their functions, use tools and equipment safely, perform hardware disassembly and assembly, install software, configure BIOS/UEFI settings, create bootable devices, and conduct computer testing.

[49] The module also introduces the basic business knowledge needed to establish a small computer repair shop, including costing, equipment planning, shop layout, and safety compliance.

[50] By completing this module, students are expected to develop technical competence, problem-solving skills, workplace discipline, and readiness for future employment or entrepreneurship in the computer servicing industry.

[301] LESSON 1 – DEFINITION OF TERMS

[302] Study these key terms before starting the lesson.

[303] Computer Systems Servicing (CSS) – The installation, maintenance, repair, configuration, and troubleshooting of computer systems and networks.

[304] Input Device – Hardware used to enter data or commands into a computer, such as a keyboard, mouse, scanner, microphone, or webcam.

[305] Output Device – Hardware that presents processed information to the user, such as a monitor, printer, speakers, or projector.

[306] Processing Device – A component that executes instructions and processes data, such as the CPU, GPU, and motherboard.

[307] Storage Device – Hardware that keeps data, such as an HDD, SSD, USB flash drive, or memory card.

[308] System Software – Software that manages the computer's hardware and provides a platform for application software.

[309] Application Software – Programs that help users perform specific tasks, such as word processing, calculation, or web browsing.

[310] Device Driver – Software that allows the operating system to communicate with a hardware device.

[311] Firmware – Low-level software that controls and manages hardware devices.

[312] Utility Software – Software that maintains and protects the computer system.

[313] Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) – Rules and practices that protect workers from accidents, injuries, and health hazards in the workplace.

[314] Hazard – Anything in the workplace that may cause injury, illness, or damage, such as electrical, ESD, physical, chemical, ergonomic, or fire hazards.

[315] Electrostatic Discharge (ESD) – A sudden transfer of static electricity that may damage sensitive electronic components.

[316] Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) – Items worn or used for protection, such as an antistatic wrist strap, ESD mat, safety goggles, gloves, and a dust mask.

[317] Digital Multimeter – A testing tool that measures voltage, resistance, and current.

[318] Power Supply Tester – A device that checks the basic output conditions of a PSU.

[319] POST Diagnostic Card – A card that displays diagnostic codes during startup to help identify motherboard or startup problems.

[320] Business Costing – The process of calculating the expenses and service charges involved in computer servicing.

[321] Labor Cost – Payment for the technician's time and skills.

[322] Parts Cost – The price of replacement components.

[323] Operating Expenses – Regular business expenses such as electricity, rent, and internet.

[324] Profit Margin – The amount added to the service price to cover business profit.

[325] Warranty – A service guarantee that may affect the price of a repair.

[326] Computer Repair Shop – A business that provides computer maintenance, troubleshooting, repair, installation, and upgrade services.

[414] Use an antistatic wrist strap connected appropriately to an ESD-safe grounding point.

[415] Never open the PSU casing because internal capacitors may retain dangerous voltage.

[418] Do not force computer components into slots.

[419] Keep screws and small parts in containers.

[420] Ensure proper ventilation in the working area.

[421] Maintain a clean and dry workspace.

[1816] 5.1 Apply BIOS/UEFI Configuration, Disk Management, and Driver Installation to Set Up a Fully Functional System

[1818] Figure 5.1. Example of a BIOS/UEFI setup screen and a motherboard used during system configuration.

[1819] BIOS (Basic Input/Output System) and UEFI (Unified Extensible Firmware Interface) are firmware interfaces that initialize hardware and help the computer locate a bootable operating system. UEFI is the modern firmware environment used by most current computers.

[1820] A. Common BIOS/UEFI Settings

[1821] • System date and time – keeps the firmware clock accurate.

[1822] • Boot priority – selects the order in which the computer checks devices for a bootable operating system.

[1823] • UEFI/Legacy or CSM mode – controls the firmware boot compatibility mode. Use the mode supported by the operating system and installation media.

[1824] • Secure Boot – helps prevent unauthorized boot software from loading. Change it only when required and authorized.

[1825] • Storage configuration – detects SATA or NVMe drives and may include controller settings. Avoid changing controller mode without understanding the effect on the installed OS.

[1826] • Hardware monitoring – displays information such as CPU temperature, fan speed, and system voltage.

[1827] B. Safe BIOS/UEFI Configuration Procedure

[1828] Read the motherboard or computer manufacturer's manual to identify the correct setup key (often Delete, F2, Esc, or a function key).

[1829] Restart the computer and press the setup key during startup.

[1830] Check that the processor, memory, and storage devices are detected.

[1831] Set the date and time if needed.

[1832] Review boot priority and select the correct UEFI USB installer when installing an OS.

[1833] Keep security and storage settings at recommended defaults unless a documented requirement calls for a change.

[1834] Save changes and exit. If the system behaves unexpectedly, return to setup and restore the previous settings or manufacturer's defaults.

[1835] C. Disk Management and Partitioning

[1836] A disk is a physical storage device such as an HDD or SSD. A partition is a logical section of a disk. A volume is a formatted storage area that the operating system can use. Disk Management in Windows can initialize disks, create and format volumes, assign drive letters, and view partition status.

[1837] • GPT (GUID Partition Table) – commonly used with UEFI systems; supports large disks and many partitions.

[1838] • MBR (Master Boot Record) – older partition style commonly associated with legacy BIOS booting.

[1839] • Formatting prepares a partition with a file system, such as NTFS. Formatting or deleting a partition can erase its contents.

[1840] D. Driver Installation

[1841] A device driver is software that allows the operating system to communicate with a hardware device. Examples include chipset, graphics, audio, LAN/Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, printer, and storage drivers.

[1842] Identify the exact computer model or motherboard model and the operating system version.

[1843] Visit the manufacturer's official support website.

[1844] Download the correct driver package for the device and operating system.

[1845] Install the driver following the manufacturer's instructions. Restart if requested.

[1846] Open Device Manager and check that the device is detected and has no relevant warning symbol.

[1847] Test the device—for example, test sound output, network connectivity, display resolution, or printer operation.

[2033] LESSON 6 – DEFINITION OF TERMS

[2034] Study these key terms before starting the lesson.

[2035] Stress Testing – Testing a computer under heavy workload to evaluate stability, temperature, and performance.

[2036] Burn-In Test – A long, controlled stress test used to check that a system remains stable.

[2037] Stability – The ability of a computer to run without errors, crashes, or restarts.

[2038] Workload – The amount of processing demand placed on a component during a test.

[2039] Thermal Throttling – The reduction of a component's performance to control temperature or power.

[2040] Overheating – A condition where components become hotter than safe limits.

[2041] Idle Temperature – The temperature of a component when the system is not under load.

[2042] Peak Temperature – The highest temperature recorded during a test.

[2043] OCCT – A tool used for stress and stability testing of the CPU, GPU, memory, and power supply.

[2044] Monitoring – Continuously watching temperatures, errors, and system behavior during a test.

[2045] Sensor Reading – A measured value such as temperature, voltage, or fan speed.

[2046] Artifacts – Visual glitches on the screen that may indicate a graphics problem.

[2047] Crash – Sudden failure of the system or a program, such as a freeze or blue screen.

[2048] Fault Isolation – Testing one component or workload at a time to find the cause of a problem.

[2049] Test Log – A written record of the test settings, times, temperatures, and observations.

[2050] Documentation – Recording procedures, results, and recommendations for future reference.

[2051] Corrective Action – The step taken to fix a problem found during testing.

[2052] Test Report – A final document stating the configuration, results, errors, conclusion, and recommendations.

[2054] 6.1 Discuss How to Apply Stress Testing Techniques to Evaluate System Performance and Stability

[2056] Figure 6.1. Example of a device driver management screen and hardware devices that may be checked before testing.

[2057] Stress testing is a method of applying a demanding workload to one or more computer components to evaluate stability, cooling, and performance. It can help reveal overheating, unstable memory, graphics errors, power problems, and system crashes. A stress test does not guarantee that a computer will never fail; it provides evidence about the system under the tested conditions.

[2058] A. Types of Stress Tests

[2059] • CPU test – places a heavy computational workload on the processor. Observe temperature, clock speed, utilization, and errors.

[2060] • GPU test – renders demanding graphics to check graphics stability, cooling, and artifacts.

[2061] • Memory test – checks RAM for errors that may cause crashes or corrupted data.

[2062] • Storage test – checks drive health, read/write behavior, and possible errors. Back up important data before any test that writes to a drive.

[2063] • Power or combined system test – loads multiple components at once and can reveal power delivery or cooling weaknesses.

[2064] B. Common Testing and Monitoring Software

[2065] • OCCT – offers CPU, GPU, memory, and power-related stability tests.

[2066] • HWiNFO – displays hardware sensors such as temperatures, fan speeds, clock speeds, and power readings.

[2067] • MemTest86 – a bootable memory diagnostic tool used to check RAM for errors.

[2068] • Manufacturer utilities – some CPU, GPU, and storage manufacturers provide their own diagnostics.

[2069] C. Preparing for a Stress Test

[2070] Confirm the computer is correctly assembled and the cooling system is working.

[2071] Check that fans spin, vents are clear, and the computer is on a stable power source.

[2072] Record the CPU, GPU, RAM, storage, operating system, and driver versions.

[2073] Close unnecessary programs and save work.

[2074] Open a monitoring utility and note idle temperatures and other baseline readings.

[2075] Choose a test that matches the component being evaluated and use a conservative duration suitable for the task.

[2076] Explain the test to the owner or supervisor and obtain permission where required.

[2077] D. Interpreting Test Results

[2078] • Pass/no reported errors – the test completed for the selected duration without reported errors or instability. This does not prove the system is fault-free.

[2079] • Overheating – temperatures approach or exceed the manufacturer's limits, or the system throttles or shuts down. Check cooler mounting, thermal interface material, airflow, and fan operation.

[2080] • Calculation or memory errors – may indicate unstable settings, faulty RAM, CPU instability, or other hardware problems. Retest methodically after checking settings.

[2081] • Artifacts or GPU errors – may indicate GPU instability, driver problems, overheating, or power issues.

[2082] • Unexpected shutdown or restart – may be related to temperature, power delivery, hardware, or software. Record the exact conditions and investigate before repeating.

[2083] E. Practical Example

[2084] A technician receives a desktop that restarts during gaming. The technician records its specifications, checks the fans and airflow, monitors idle temperatures, and runs a controlled GPU test while observing temperature and system behavior. If the system restarts, the technician stops the test, records the time and readings, checks power and cooling, and performs further diagnosis rather than repeatedly stressing the system.

[2085] 6.2 Perform a Stress or Burning Test on a Computer System, Record the Results, and Analyze Any Issues Observed During Testing

[2087] Figure 6.2. Example of a desktop computer under a controlled CPU/GPU workload with utilization and temperature monitoring.

[2088] A burn-in test is a period of sustained operation under a selected workload. It is used to check whether a computer remains stable over time. A technician should choose an appropriate test duration, monitor the system continuously, and document the results.

[2089] A. Equipment and Software

[2090] • Computer under test with working CPU cooler, GPU cooling, case fans, and power supply.

[2091] • Monitor, keyboard, and mouse.

[2092] • OCCT or another reputable component-specific test utility.

[2093] • HWiNFO or a similar sensor-monitoring utility.

[2094] • A test record sheet or spreadsheet.

[2095] B. Step-by-Step Stress/Burn-In Test Procedure

[2096] Obtain permission and confirm that important work is saved.

[2097] Record the computer model and specifications, operating system, driver versions, and date.

[2098] Inspect the case, fans, cooler, cables, and vents. Do not open the power supply unit.

[2099] Start the computer and allow it to reach a normal idle state.

[2100] Open the monitoring software and record baseline CPU/GPU temperatures, fan speeds, and utilization.

[2101] Select the appropriate test (CPU, GPU, memory, or combined) and set a conservative test duration based on the task and hardware guidance.

[2102] Start the test and continuously observe temperatures, utilization, fan behavior, error messages, and system stability.

[2103] Stop the test immediately if unsafe temperatures, errors, unusual noise or smell, artifacts, or instability occurs.

[2104] At the end of the test, stop the workload normally and save the software report or screenshots.

[2105] Compare the results with manufacturer guidance and the baseline readings. Identify possible causes of any issue.

[2106] Write a conclusion and recommendations, including whether the system passed the selected test conditions or needs further diagnosis.

[2108] Note: The table is a blank-style example. Students must enter the actual readings from their own supervised test. Do not copy sample readings as real results.

[2109] D. Sample Analysis of Observed Issues

[2110] • If the CPU temperature rises rapidly and the computer throttles, inspect the cooler installation, fan operation, airflow, and thermal interface material.

[2111] • If memory errors appear, record the test and settings, then check RAM seating, supported memory settings, and individual modules using an appropriate diagnostic procedure.

[2112] • If the GPU displays artifacts, check GPU temperature, driver version, power connectors, and stability at default settings.

[2113] • If the system unexpectedly shuts down, stop testing and investigate cooling, power supply, cables, and hardware condition.

[2114] E. Documentation Requirements

[2115] • Computer identification and hardware specifications.

[2116] • Test software and version, selected test type, settings, and duration.

[2117] • Start and end time, idle and peak temperatures, and relevant sensor readings.

[2118] • Errors, crashes, artifacts, throttling, unusual noises, or other observations.

[2119] • Final result, interpretation, corrective action, and technician's name/signature.

[2561] Lesson 1 — Definition of Terms

[2562] Study these key terms before starting the lesson.

[2564] 1.1 Understand the network before installing

[2565] A network allows computers and other devices to exchange data and share resources. Before installation, identify the purpose of the network, the number and location of devices, the available internet connection, the required coverage, and the safest cable route.

[2567] Figure 1. Example SOHO layout. The router connects the local network to the internet; the switch expands the number of wired connections; an access point provides Wi-Fi.

[2568] 1.2 Common network components

[2569] Modem/ONT: terminates the service-provider connection. Some units combine modem/ONT and router functions.

[2571] Figure 1.1. An image of a modem.

[2572] A modem or ONT usually looks like a small rectangular electronic box with indicator lights and ports for connecting cables.

[2574] Router: connects the LAN to other networks and commonly provides DHCP, NAT, firewall, and Wi-Fi functions.

[2577] Figure 1.2. An image of a router.

[2579] A router is the device that connects your local network (LAN) to other networks, usually the Internet. It directs data between devices and commonly provides DHCP, NAT, firewall, and Wi-Fi functions.

[2581] Switch: connects multiple wired devices in one LAN.

[2584] Figure 1.3.  An image of what looks like a switch.

[2586] A network switch is a device that connects multiple wired devices within the same Local Area Network (LAN). Think of it as an expansion device that gives your network more Ethernet ports.

[2588] Wireless access point: provides Wi-Fi access to the LAN. A router may already contain an access point.

[2591] Figure 1.4. An image of a wireless access point.

[2593] A wireless access point (WAP) is a networking device that allows wireless devices, such as laptops, smartphones, and tablets, to connect to a wired Local Area Network (LAN) through Wi-Fi.

[2595] Think of it as a device that adds Wi-Fi connectivity to an existing wired network.

[2597] Note: A wireless access point (WAP) and a router are different networking devices, although they can look very similar. They can even look identical because modern networking equipment often combines multiple functions into one device.

[2599] NIC: the Ethernet or Wi-Fi interface installed in a computer or device.

[2602] Figure 1.5. An image of NIC.

[2604] An NIC (Network Interface Card) is a hardware component that allows a computer or device to connect to a network.

[2606] Think of it as the computer's communication equipment. Without an NIC, a computer cannot communicate with other devices over a network.

[2608] Ethernet cable: carries network data between wired devices.

[2611] Figure 1.6. An image of ethernet cable.

[2613] An Ethernet cable is a physical network cable used to connect devices, such as computers, routers, and switches, so they can communicate and exchange data over a network.

[2615] Think of it as a road that carries information between network devices.

[2617] 1.3 Materials, tools, and safety

[2618] Typical materials include Cat5e or Cat6 UTP cable, RJ-45 plugs compatible with the cable, keystone jacks or wall plates, cable clips/raceway.

[2620] Typical tools include a cable cutter/stripper, RJ-45 crimping tool, punch-down tool (for keystone jacks), cable tester.

[2622] Safety checklist: obtain permission; inspect the work area; avoid wet locations and trip hazards; do not run data cable where it may be crushed or sharply bent; keep appropriate separation from power wiring; use tools correctly; secure ladders and wear eye protection when needed; follow housekeeping practices and dispose of offcuts properly.

[2623] 1.4 Ethernet cable and T568B termination

[2624] Twisted-pair Ethernet cable contains four pairs of insulated copper conductors. The twists help reduce interference. For a common straight-through patch cable, both ends use the same wiring standard. T568B is widely used; follow the standard specified by the site or instructor.

[2626] Figure 1.13. T568B order, pins 1–8: White/Orange, Orange, White/Green, Blue, White/Blue, Green, White/Brown, Brown.

[2628] Figure 1.14. Wiring diagram

[2630] Example: making a straight-through cable

[2631] Measure the required cable length, allowing a little slack without creating a coil or trip hazard.

[2632] Cut the cable squarely. Strip only enough outer jacket to arrange the pairs without damaging insulation.

[2633] Untwist and arrange the eight conductors in T568B order. Keep untwisting to the minimum needed.

[2634] Trim the conductors evenly, insert them fully into the RJ-45 plug, and check that the jacket is captured by the plug's strain-relief area.

[2635] Crimp the plug using the correct tool. Repeat the same pinout at the other end.

[2636] Test the cable with a cable tester. Confirm that pins 1 through 8 map correctly and that there are no opens, shorts, or crossed conductors.

[2637] Label the cable and install it along the planned route using suitable clips or raceway.

[2638] Important: A basic continuity tester can identify many wiring faults, but it does not certify cable performance at a particular Ethernet category or data rate.

[2819] 2.1 IPv4 settings

[2820] For devices to communicate on an IP network, they need compatible network settings. The key IPv4 settings are IP address, subnet mask, default gateway, and DNS server.

[2821] Example network: Router LAN address 192.168.1.1; subnet mask 255.255.255.0; PC 1 address 192.168.1.10; PC 2 address 192.168.1.11. These addresses are examples only. Check the actual network before assigning addresses and avoid conflicts.

[2822] 2.2 DHCP versus static addressing

[2823] DHCP automatically leases IP settings to clients and is suitable for most ordinary workstations. Static addressing manually assigns a fixed address and is often used for infrastructure devices or equipment that must keep a known address. A manually assigned address must not conflict with another device or the DHCP pool.

[3465] Student ID is a unique identifier in the database and is required by the backend to disambiguate duplicate Full Name + Program matches. If the login flow requires it, reveal the Student ID field only when the backend indicates that multiple matches exist; do not invent a client-side matching rule.

[3466] Students do not have email/password credentials in the supplied design. Do not add student self-registration or a student password reset flow.

[3467] Admin login fields: username and password. Never store or display the Admin password; the backend stores only a password hash.

[3468] Show clear validation, inactive-account, ambiguous-match, rate-limit, and session-expired messages based on backend responses. Do not reveal whether unrelated student records exist.

[3469] 4. Course navigation and learning structure

[3470] Render the hierarchy as Competency → Discussion → Topics/Subtopics. A Discussion is the learning unit; its topics/subtopics are presented inside that Discussion. Do not create a separate seven-stage sequence for every topic.

[3486] 6. Learning-stage screen behavior

[3487] Pre-test: show instructions and questions; allow submission even if the score is low. After submission, show score and incorrectly answered questions; the system design says correct answers may also be shown.

[3488] Definition of Terms: render the Admin-managed glossary/content body. Mark complete only after the learner completes the stage according to the agreed interaction (for example, an explicit Mark as Complete action or a backend completion action).

[3489] Explanation: render the main Discussion explanation and its topic/subtopic content. Support readable text, headings, lists, and inline images as provided by the content body.

[3490] Examples: render example text and images in sequence; provide responsive image sizing and descriptive alternative text where available.

[3491] Video Lessons: render supported embedded video or an external link, title/source, and fallback link. Do not assume every video provider supports embedding.

[3492] Activity: display instructions, materials/equipment, safety reminders, and task steps. Submit answers/evidence using the configured activity format. After submission, show score, incorrect answers, and correct answers. A passing score is not required.

[3493] Discussion Post-test: submit once. Show score, incorrect answers, and correct answers after submission. Passing is not required to complete the Discussion.

[3494] The database stores one editable content body per stage and Discussion in learning_stage_contents. The assessment questions/options/answer keys are stored separately in assessments, questions, and answer_choices. The stage content body is not a substitute for assessment question records.

[3495] 7. Assessment UI rules

[3496] Discussion assessments are Pre-test, Activity, and Discussion Post-test. Each has one attempt record per student. An IN_PROGRESS attempt may be resumed; once SUBMITTED, the student cannot start another attempt for that same Discussion assessment.

[3497] Save draft responses as the learner answers when the backend supports it. On reload, fetch the existing IN_PROGRESS attempt and restore saved responses; do not create a duplicate attempt.

[3498] The front end sends responses only. It must not calculate authoritative scores, pass/fail outcomes, correct-answer flags, or completion status.

[3499] After submission, display the backend-returned score and feedback. Do not expose correct_answer_data or answer keys in question payloads before the allowed feedback point.

[3500] Generalized Post-tests are separate competency-level assessments: one for COC 1 and one for COC 2. Each starts with three submissions, requires at least 70% to pass, and each Admin retake authorization adds another block of three attempts.

[3501] Show attempts used/remaining and PASSED/FAILED/LOCKED status using backend data. Only submitted attempts consume the allowance. Do not decrement attempts locally before a confirmed submission response.

[3502] A passed competency stays passed. Keep previous Generalized Post-test results visible in history where the API permits.

[3503] 8. Progression and unlocking behavior

[3504] The first Discussion is available only when its competency is available.

[3505] The next Discussion unlocks only after all seven stages of the previous Discussion are COMPLETED.

[3506] The Generalized Post-test becomes available only after all Discussions in that competency are complete and the backend confirms an attempt is available.

[3507] COC 2 remains locked until the student passes the COC 1 Generalized Post-test (at least 70%).

[3508] COC 2 progression and its Generalized Post-test follow the same stage, assessment, and attempt rules.

[3509] Display locked-state explanations such as “Complete the previous Discussion” or “Pass the COC 1 Generalized Post-test to unlock COC 2,” based on backend status.

[3510] Progress percentages should be derived from completed stage rows returned by the backend, not maintained as an independent front-end counter. Refresh progress from the server after each successful stage completion or assessment submission.

[3511] 9. Admin screens and content-management behavior

[3512] Dashboard: show useful counts and links to students, course content, results, and pending retake actions.

[3513] Student Management: list/search students; create, edit, activate/deactivate records; collect Student ID, Full Name, and Program. Preserve historical records when deactivating.

[3514] Course Management: manage the two competencies, ordered Discussions, and ordered topics/subtopics. Follow the approved COC structure; avoid deleting records that have progress or assessment history.

[3515] Learning Content Management: select competency → Discussion → stage; edit the stage title and single content body. Support text and media URLs for definitions, explanations, examples, and videos.

[3516] Assessment Management: select the assessment scope/type; create and order questions and answer choices; configure points and question type; maintain answer keys in the Admin-only interface.

[3517] Results/Progress: filter by student, competency, Discussion, and assessment; show stage status, submitted attempts, scores, and timestamps.

[3518] Retake Management: show failed/locked Generalized Post-tests; allow an authorized Admin to grant a new three-attempt block; record the grant and preserve previous attempts.

[3519] The supplied database design uses separate tables for admins, students, competencies, discussions, topics, learning_stage_contents, assessments, questions, answer_choices, assessment_attempts, attempt_answers, student_stage_progress, student_competency_progress, and retake_authorizations. Use backend APIs for all CRUD operations; do not hard-code course content into React components.

[3520] 10. Front-end data handling and API integration

[3521] Create one centralized API client with the configured base URL, credentials/session-cookie behavior, common error handling, and request/response typing.

[3522] Keep API calls in service modules (for example, auth, competencies, discussions, learning content, assessments, progress, admin). Keep page components focused on rendering and interaction.

[3523] Use backend IDs as stable identifiers. Use display_order to render competencies, Discussions, topics, stages, questions, and answer choices in the intended sequence.

[3524] Fetch current user/session and role before rendering protected routes. Fetch course/progress data from the backend rather than duplicating it in static arrays.

[3525] Represent loading, empty, success, validation-error, unauthorized, forbidden, and server-error states on every data-driven screen.

[3526] After create/update/deactivate actions, refresh or update the relevant server-backed list only after the backend confirms success.

[3527] Never place database credentials, password hashes, correct answer keys, or secret environment values in frontend source or browser-visible configuration.

[3528] Use pagination/search where result lists may grow. Avoid fetching every student's full assessment history on the initial dashboard.

[3529] The supplied design documents define the data model and behaviors but do not provide finalized REST endpoint paths or JSON payload contracts. Agree those contracts with the Spring Boot developer before wiring screens; do not invent endpoint URLs or assume response field names.

[3530] 11. UI quality, accessibility, and responsive behavior

[3531] Use a consistent responsive layout for mobile, tablet, and desktop. Student lesson pages should remain readable on narrow screens.

[3532] Use clear labels, visible focus states, keyboard-operable controls, sufficient color contrast, and semantic headings.

[3533] Do not communicate LOCKED/AVAILABLE/COMPLETED or PASSED/FAILED using color alone; include text or icons with accessible labels.

[3534] Provide image alternative text, video fallback links, and clear confirmation before destructive Admin actions.

[3535] Prevent accidental double submission by disabling the submit action while a request is pending; still rely on backend idempotency/transaction rules.
